# Comparative Analysis of Lithium-Ion and Sodium-Ion Battery Chemistries: Electrochemical Performance, Supply Chain Economics, Thermal Safety, and Industrial Scale

## Introduction & Context

The global electrochemical energy storage paradigm is undergoing a structural shift driven by the imperative for supply chain resilience, raw material security, and long-term cost stabilization [4, 14]. While Lithium-Ion Batteries (LIBs)—specifically Lithium Iron Phosphate ($\text{LiFePO}_4$ / LFP) and Nickel Manganese Cobalt Oxide ($\text{LiNi}_x\text{Mn}_y\text{Co}_z\text{O}_2$ / NMC) chemistries—have historically dominated mobile electric mobility and stationary energy storage markets, material supply concentration and severe price volatility associated with lithium carbonate, cobalt, and nickel have accelerated the commercialization of alternative platform chemistries [4, 12, 14].

Sodium-Ion Batteries (SIBs) have emerged as the primary complementary technology to LIBs [3, 13]. Operating on an open-circuit voltage and intercalation framework analogous to the traditional "rocking-chair" ion transfer mechanism, SIBs leverage the high elemental abundance and geographic dispersion of sodium precursors to provide an alternate path away from critical mineral supply chains [4, 14]. Mass-production validation has demonstrated cell-level energy densities reaching up to 175 Wh/kg alongside production cost corridors of $52–$65/kWh [3]. Furthermore, the close architectural alignment between SIBs and LIBs enables existing gigafactories to pivot to SIB manufacturing with minimal equipment capital expenditure [11].

However, translating lab-scale validation to widespread industrial adoption involves navigating distinct market and technical hurdles. System-level SIB storage tenders demonstrate a ~94% price premium over mature LFP systems despite lower cell-level production costs, reflecting early-stage supply chain integration and scaling inefficiencies [3]. Additionally, while SIBs exhibit reduced low-temperature lithium-plating hazards, elevated operating temperatures intensify metallic sodium reactivity, introducing distinct degradation pathways that require specialized thermo-electrochemical safety engineering [7, 9, 10]. This report provides a comprehensive comparative analysis of SIB and LIB technologies across their core electrochemical metrics, economic architectures, thermal safety profiles, degradation kinetics, and industrial deployment trajectories.

### Gravimetric and Volumetric Energy Density
The fundamental electrochemical divergence between SIBs and LIBs stems from the ionic radii and standard reduction potentials of sodium ($\text{Na}^+$) versus lithium ($\text{Li}^+$). Sodium possesses a larger ionic radius (1.02 Å vs. 0.76 Å for $\text{Li}^+$) and a higher standard reduction potential (-2.71 V vs. SHE compared to -3.04 V for $\text{Li}^+$), which inherently constrains the theoretical volumetric energy density and nominal operating cell voltage of SIB chemistries [1, 6]. 

Current mass-production SIB cells achieve a gravimetric energy density benchmark of 175 Wh/kg [3]. This performance corridor directly overlaps with entry-level LFP formulations [1]. However, SIBs remain lower in volumetric energy density (~250–350 Wh/L) compared to high-density LIB chemistries such as NMC (~500–700 Wh/L) and LFP (~350–450 Wh/L), as the larger ionic radius of $\text{Na}^+$ demands expanded host lattice structures within cathode intercalation materials—such as Prussian Blue Analogues (PBAs) or Layered Transition Metal Oxides ($\text{Na}_x\text{MO}_2$)—and hard carbon anodes [1, 6, 15].

```
+---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                                CATHODE INTERCALATION DYNAMICS                                           |
+---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  Sodium Intercalation (SIBs):                                                                           |
|  Na+ (1.02 Å)  <===>  Expanded Lattice Hosts (Layered Oxides / PBAs) [1, 15]                             |
|  • Requires larger interstitial volumes; leads to greater lattice strain during cycling [15].          |
|                                                                                                         |
|  Lithium Intercalation (LIBs):                                                                          |
|  Li+ (0.76 Å)  <===>  Compact Olivine / Layered Structures (LFP / NMC) [1]                              |
|  • Supports tighter packing density and higher volumetric capacity [1].                                |
+---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

### Rate Capability and Interphase Kinetics
Despite volumetric energy density limitations, SIBs exhibit favorable charge-transfer kinetics under specific operating regimes [2, 6, 16]. Standardized efficiency modeling indicates that the lower desolvation energy of $\text{Na}^+$ ions in select non-aqueous organic solvents (e.g., ester-based or ether-based electrolytes) enables fast interfacial charge transfer relative to standard LFP formulations [2, 6, 16]. Component-level analytical modeling tools, such as **CellEst 3.0**, demonstrate that SIB electrode configurations achieve rapid ion transport across the Solid-Electrolyte Interphase (SEI), supporting high-rate charging capabilities without inducing severe polarization losses [5, 6, 16].

The interfacial kinetics during charge transfer can be represented by the activation energy barrier for desolvation ($\Delta E_{\text{desolv}}$):

$$\text{Rate of Interfacial Transfer} \propto \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta E_{\text{desolv}}}{R \cdot T}\right)$$

Because $\text{Na}^+$ exhibits a weaker Lewis acidity than $\text{Li}^+$, its solvation sheath is less tightly bound, yielding a reduced $\Delta E_{\text{desolv}}$ and facilitating rapid ion transport across the electrode-electrolyte boundary [6, 16].

### Cycle Life and Environmental Temperature Dependence
The lifecycle performance of SIBs is governed by the structural stability of hard carbon anodes and cathode host matrices during repeated sodium insertion and extraction [1, 9, 15, 17]. SIBs show high capacity retention across extended cycling (typically 3,000–6,000 cycles depending on cathode selection and depth of discharge), though they generally trail ultra-long-life LFP cells designed for grid storage, which can exceed 8,000–10,000 cycles [1, 2, 17]. 

Where SIBs exhibit a clear operational advantage is in sub-zero environments. While traditional LIBs suffer severe capacity fade, mass transfer resistance, and lithium plating risks below 0°C, SIBs maintain high discharge capacity retention (>80% at -20°C and >70% at -40°C) and superior rate capability under severe cold conditions [1, 3, 12].

## Raw Material Abundance, Supply Chain Geopolitics, and Manufacturing Costs

### Material Security and Resource Abundance
The primary economic driver for SIB commercialization is the geographic concentration and cost volatility of raw materials required for LIB production [4, 14]. Lithium, cobalt, and nickel extraction carries significant geopolitical exposure, supply bottleneck risks, and environmental degradation concerns [4, 14]. In contrast, sodium is the sixth most abundant element in the Earth's crust and is globally distributed, sourced from inexpensive precursor salts such as soda ash ($\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$) or sodium chloride ($\text{NaCl}$) [4, 14].

Furthermore, SIBs eliminate the requirement for copper current collectors on the anode side. Because sodium does not form alloys with aluminum at low potentials (unlike lithium, which forms $\text{Li}\text{Al}$ alloys below 0.6 V vs. $\text{Li}/\text{Li}^+$), SIBs utilize inexpensive aluminum foil for both cathode and anode current collectors [4, 11]. This substitution mitigates copper supply constraints, reduces cell mass, and lowers cell-level raw material expenses [4, 5].

```

|                                SUPPLY CHAIN & ECONOMIC PARAMETERS                                       |
+------------------------------+------------------------------------+-------------------------------------+
| Economic / Supply Dimension  | Sodium-Ion Batteries (SIBs)        | Lithium-Ion Batteries (LFP)         |
+------------------------------+------------------------------------+-------------------------------------+
| Anode Current Collector      | Aluminum Foil (Lower Cost) [4, 11] | Copper Foil (Higher Cost) [4, 5]    |
| Primary Precursors           | Abundant Sodium Salts / Soda Ash [4]| Lithium Carbonate / Hydroxide [4]   |
| Critical Minerals Needed     | Zero Co, Ni, or Li required [4, 14]| Requires Li, Fe, P (or Ni/Co) [4]   |
| Cell Manufacturing Cost      | $52 – $65 / kWh [3]                | $50 – $70 / kWh (Market dependent)[3|
```

### Industrial Scale and Geographic Concentration
Global SIB industrial manufacturing capacity is heavily concentrated in China, which leads commercial scaling with 293.7 GWh of planned SIB cell manufacturing capacity [3]. This industrial commitment is supported by over 100 GWh in signed global energy storage framework orders [3]. Key industrial enterprises driving mass commercialization include CATL, Faradion, HiNa Battery, and Tiamat [13]. These entities are establishing integrated supply chains for hard carbon anodes, layered oxide and PBA cathodes, and specialized sodium electrolytes [13].

### Manufacturing Cost Corridors vs. System Tender Paradox
Component-level economic modeling via tools like CellEst 3.0 projects that mature SIB cell manufacturing costs range between **$52 and $65 per kWh** [3, 5]. This structural cost floor reflects raw material savings achieved by substituting lithium for sodium and copper for aluminum [4, 5].

```
+---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                                CELL VS. SYSTEM COST PARADOX                                            |
+---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| Cell Manufacturing Level:  [ SIB Cell: $52–$65/kWh ]  ≈  [ LFP Cell: Baseline Market Cost ] [3, 5]     |
|                                                                                                         |
| System Integration Level:  [ SIB System Tenders ]    --- (+94% Price Premium) ---> [ LFP Systems ] [3]  |
|                                                                                                         |
| Primary Drivers: Balance-of-Plant (BOP) immature integration, supply chain scaling lag, early pricing.  |
+---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

However, real-world market deployment reveals an economic paradox:
* **The System Pricing Gap:** Despite favorable cell production costs, commercial SIB stationary storage system tenders reflect a **~94% price premium** over established LFP storage systems [3].
* **Underlying Drivers:** This disparity stems from an immature balance-of-plant (BOP) integration ecosystem, unoptimized pack energy packing efficiencies, limited long-term field degradation data for risk underwriting, and strategic supplier margin maintenance during initial commercial rollouts [3, 12, 17].
* **Lithium Price Sensitivity:** When global lithium carbonate market prices drop below ~$12,000/metric ton, SIBs struggle to achieve total system cost parity against mature LFP systems, restricting immediate adoption to specific environmental niches unless scale economies lower system-level integration costs [12].

## Safety Profiles, Thermal Stability, and Degradation Mechanisms Under Extreme Operating Conditions

### Interphase Kinetics and State-of-Charge (SOC) Runaway Dynamics
The thermal safety profile of SIBs is governed by the thermo-electrochemical dynamics of the Solid-Electrolyte Interphase (SEI) on hard carbon anodes and the chemical decomposition dynamics of cathode host structures [6, 8, 17]. The thermal runaway propensity of SIB cells is strongly dependent on the State-of-Charge (SOC) [8]:

$$\text{Thermal Hazard Severity} = f(\text{SOC}, T_{\text{onset}}, \Delta H_{\text{decomposition}})$$

As SOC increases, the thermodynamic instability of the fully sodiated hard carbon anode ($\text{Na}_x\text{C}_6$) and deintercalated cathode accelerates heat-generation rates during thermal abuse events [6, 8]. Standardized safety assessments indicate that SIBs exhibit higher initial thermal decomposition onset temperatures ($T_{\text{onset}} \approx 190\text{--}220^\circ\text{C}$) than NMC chemistries ($T_{\text{onset}} \approx 150\text{--}170^\circ\text{C}$), providing a wider margin of passive thermal safety under moderate operational abuse [2, 6, 8].

Furthermore, the activation energy for initial Solid-Electrolyte Interphase breakdown in sodium cells differs substantially from lithium analogs due to the higher solubility and inorganic salt composition (e.g., $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$, $\text{NaHCO}_3$, and $\text{NaF}$) of the sodium SEI layer [6, 16]. Detailed differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and accelerating rate calorimetry (ARC) studies confirm that while the total enthalpy of decomposition ($\Delta H_{\text{decomposition}}$) at 100% SOC approaches levels comparable to LFP, the self-heating rate ($dT/dt$) during early-stage thermal failure proceeds at a lower initial ramp rate in SIBs [6, 8]. This kinetic deceleration provides battery management systems (BMS) extended reaction windows for active fault isolation before self-sustaining exothermic propagation occurs [8, 10].

```
+---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                                THERMAL RUNAWAY & DEGRADATION MECHANISMS                                 |
+------------------------------+------------------------------------+-------------------------------------+
| Operating Condition          | Sodium-Ion Cells (SIBs)            | Lithium-Ion Cells (LIBs)            |
+------------------------------+------------------------------------+-------------------------------------+
| Overcharge / Fast Charge     | Reduced dendrite formation risk;   | High risk of Lithium Plating;       |
|                              | higher threshold for shorting [7]  | internal thermal shorting [7]       |
| High Temp (>50°C)            | Accelerated Metallic Na reactivity;| SEI breakdown, transition metal     |
|                              | rapid capacity fade & gas [7, 9]   | dissolution, thermal runaway [6, 7] |
| Deep Discharge (0V Transport)| Safe down to 0V without current    | Copper dissolution damages cell     |
|                              | collector dissolution (Al foil) [4]| permanently (requires min SOC) [4, 5|
+------------------------------+------------------------------------+-------------------------------------+
```

### Thermal Runaway Pathways and Abuse Testing Profiles
To rigorously evaluate the operational safety of SIBs versus LIBs, standard industry abuse protocols (including UL 9540A, UN 38.3, and SAE J2464) submit cells to mechanical, electrical, and thermal stress regimes [2, 8, 10]:

1. **Nail Penetration and Mechanical Impact:** Under mechanical intrusion testing, SIB cells demonstrate delayed thermal runaway onset relative to high-density NMC cells [8, 10]. The lower volumetric energy density and lower local short-circuit current density reduce the instantaneous enthalpy surge, mitigating catastrophic explosive rupture [8, 10].
2. **Electrical Overcharge Stress:** Under overcharge abuse (>150% SOC), SIBs undergo initial electrolyte oxidation and gas evolution ($\text{CO}_2$, $\text{CO}$, $\text{C}_2\text{H}_4$). However, because $\text{Na}^+$ deposition on hard carbon features a lower structural tendency toward needle-like dendritic growth than lithium on graphite under standard rates, sudden short-circuiting induced by metallic dendrites is delayed [7, 8].
3. **Thermal Propagation in Modules:** In multi-cell pack configurations, the lower self-heating rate ($dT/dt$) of SIBs reduces the risk of cell-to-cell thermal propagation [8, 10]. Standard fire suppression media and passive phase-change thermal barriers are highly effective at preventing cascading thermal runaway in SIB racks [10].

### High-Temperature Metallic Sodium Reactivity vs. Dendrite Mitigation
Under extreme operating conditions, the degradation pathways of SIBs diverge from traditional LIBs:

1. **Dendrite and Plating Kinetics:** SIBs exhibit a lower propensity for dendrite-induced short-circuiting during rapid charge cycles at low temperatures, as sodium deposition mechanics differ from lithium plating dynamics due to distinct surface energy and self-diffusion barriers [7].
2. **High-Temperature Instability:** At elevated temperatures (>50°C), the suppression of dendrite formation is offset by a marked increase in the chemical reactivity of metallic sodium [7, 9]. Unbound or parasitic metallic sodium reacts aggressively with organic carbonate solvent molecules in the electrolyte [7, 9].
3. **Capacity Fading Mechanisms:** High-temperature exposure accelerates SEI dissolution and continuous re-passivation, resulting in gas generation, impedance growth, active sodium inventory loss, and rapid capacity fading [7, 9, 10, 17]. The chemical degradation reaction at high temperatures can be expressed as:

$$\text{Na}_x\text{C}_6 + \text{Electrolyte Organic Solvents} \xrightarrow{\Delta, T > 50^\circ\text{C}} \text{SEI Decomposition Products} + \text{Gas Generation} (\text{C}_2\text{H}_4, \text{CO}_2) + \text{Heat}$$

4. **Multi-Scale Safety Engineering:** Mitigating high-temperature degradation requires targeted safety interventions across multiple scales—including non-flammable fluorinated electrolyte additives, atomic layer deposition (ALD) surface coatings on cathode materials, and active pack-level liquid cooling propagation barriers [7, 10].

### Zero-Volt Transportation Safety
A major logistic and safety advantage of SIBs is their ability to be stored and transported at zero volts (0V State-of-Charge) [4, 11]. Because SIBs utilize aluminum current collectors on both the positive and negative electrodes, the anode can be fully discharged to 0V without inducing current collector dissolution [4, 5]. Conversely, discharging standard LIBs below critical voltage thresholds (~1.5V) causes copper anode collector dissolution into the electrolyte, forming copper dendrites that cause internal short circuits and catastrophic thermal failure upon recharge [4, 5]. Transporting SIBs at 0V eliminates thermal runaway risks during shipping, maritime transit, and warehouse storage [4, 11].

## Gigafactory Infrastructure Reusability and Commercial Use-Case Divergence

### Drop-In Gigafactory Retrofitting Compatibility
The rapid scaling potential of SIB technology is supported by its structural compatibility with existing LIB gigafactory manufacturing infrastructure [11]. Roll-to-roll manufacturing steps—including active material slurry mixing, double-sided coating, calendering, slitting, cell winding/stacking, electrolyte injection, and cell formation—are operationally equivalent between the two chemistries [11].

```
+---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                                 GIGAFACTORY RETROFITTING FLOWCHART                                      |
+---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  [ Slurry Mixing ] ---> [ Roll-to-Roll Coating ] ---> [ Calendering & Slitting ] ---> [ Cell Assembly ]  |
|         │                        │                             │                         │              |
|         ▼                        ▼                             ▼                         ▼              |
|   100% Compatible          100% Compatible               100% Compatible           100% Compatible        |
| (Hard Carbon / Na+ Salts)  (Al Foil Both Sides)         (Standard Tooling)        (Winding/Stacking)     |
|                                                                                                         |
|  * Retrofit Requirement: Minor adjustments to drying protocols, electrolyte dosing, & formation profiles. |
+---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

Converting a modern LIB gigafactory line to produce SIB cells requires only minor process modifications [11]:
* **Slurry & Coating Line Adjustments:** Adapting mixing shear rates for hard carbon anode binder formulations and switching the negative electrode collector feed line from copper to aluminum foil [4, 11].
* **Electrolyte Injection Protocols:** Adjusting dosing equipment to accommodate the viscosity, wetting kinetics, and salt compositions of sodium-based electrolyte formulations [6, 11, 16].
* **Formation & Aging Protocols:** Reconfiguring automated formation cyclers to account for SIB SEI-building voltage profiles and deep 0V discharge conditioning [5, 11].

This drop-in capability allows battery manufacturers to pivot production lines between LIB and SIB cells in response to raw material spot price fluctuations without incurring stranded capital expenditure [11, 12].

### Divergent Optimal Commercial Use Cases
Despite manufacturing similarities, SIBs and LIBs serve different primary commercial application profiles based on their performance trade-offs [3, 12].

```
+---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                                COMMERCIAL USE-CASE DIVERGENCE                                           |
+------------------------------+------------------------------------+-------------------------------------+
| Target Application           | Sodium-Ion Batteries (SIBs)        | Lithium-Ion Batteries (LIBs)        |
+------------------------------+------------------------------------+-------------------------------------+
| Cold-Climate EVs             | Highly Optimal: Superior sub-zero  | Sub-Optimal: Severe range loss;     |
|                              | capacity & rate retention [3, 12]  | requires energy-intensive heating   |
| Stationary Energy Storage    | Highly Optimal (Long-term):        | Currently Dominant: Lower system    |
| (BESS / Grid Infrastructure) | High safety, low material cost [3] | tender costs at scale today [3, 12] |
| Premium / Long-Range EVs     | Unsuitable: Lower volumetric       | Dominant: High volumetric energy    |
|                              | energy density limits range [1, 3] | density (NMC/High-Silicon) [1, 3]   |
| Light Micro-Mobility         | Optimal: Replaces lead-acid and    | Competes on energy density;         |
| (2/3-Wheelers, Forklifts)    | entry-level LFP safely [3, 13]     | higher cost sensitive baseline [12] |
+------------------------------+------------------------------------+-------------------------------------+
```

1. **Stationary Energy Storage Systems (BESS):** SIBs are well-suited for stationary grid storage, renewable integration, and microgrids where volumetric footprint constraints are secondary to material availability, thermal tolerance, safety, and levelized cost of storage (LCOS) [3, 12].
2. **Cold-Climate Electric Vehicles:** The low-temperature performance of SIBs makes them ideal for short-to-medium range electric passenger vehicles and commercial fleets operating in cold geographic regions, where LFP batteries suffer severe range degradation and charging limitations [3, 12].
3. **Micro-Mobility and Urban Telecom Power:** SIBs are rapidly replacing lead-acid and entry-level LFP units in electric two/three-wheelers, industrial forklifts, and telecom backup systems, leveraging their 0V shipping safety and rapid charge rates [3, 13].
4. **Premium EV Limitations:** SIBs are not suitable replacements for long-range electric passenger vehicles, where high volumetric energy density (enabled by nickel-dense NMC and silicon-composite anodes) remains a primary design constraint [1, 3].

### Strategic Synthesis
The comparative evaluation of Lithium-Ion and Sodium-Ion battery platforms demonstrates that SIBs serve as a viable complementary technology rather than an immediate wholesale replacement for high-density LIBs [1, 3, 12]. 

```
+---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                                   STRATEGIC COMPARISON SYNTHESIS                                        |
+---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
| Key Strengths of SIBs:                                                                                  |
|  • Raw Material Resilience: Relies on abundant Na salts and aluminum collectors (no Cu/Li/Co/Ni) [4, 14]. |
|  • Drop-in Manufacturing: Utilizes existing LIB gigafactories with minor tooling updates [11].          |
|  • Low-Temperature & Safety: High sub-zero efficiency, low dendrite risk, 0V shipping capability [3, 4, 7]|
|                                                                                                         |
| Primary Adoption Barriers:                                                                              |
|  • System Tender Cost Paradox: System tenders remain ~94% higher than LFP despite lower cell costs [3].|
|  • Thermal Degradation at High Temp: Accelerated metallic Na reactivity limits uncooled profiles [7, 9].|
|  • Volumetric Density Thresholds: Constrains long-range EV applicability [1, 3].                         |
+---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

1. **Supply Chain Isolation:** SIBs provide a hedge against lithium, cobalt, and nickel supply bottlenecks [4, 14]. By utilizing abundant sodium precursors and eliminating copper anode collectors in favor of aluminum, SIBs decouple battery manufacturing from critical mineral geopolitics [4, 5, 14].
2. **Manufacturing Flexibility:** The drop-in compatibility of SIBs within existing LIB gigafactories reduces capital risks for cell manufacturers [11]. Production lines can be reconfigured between chemistries depending on prevailing raw material spot prices [11, 12].
3. **Resolving the System Pricing Paradox:** Closing the ~94% cost gap between cell-level projections ($52–$65/kWh) and commercial system tender prices requires standardized balance-of-plant (BOP) architecture, optimized thermal pack designs, and accumulated field reliability data for financial underwriting [3, 5, 17].
4. **Thermo-Electrochemical Management:** While SIBs mitigate low-temperature lithium plating and enable safe 0V transportation, high-temperature degradation mechanisms driven by metallic sodium reactivity require dedicated thermal management systems [4, 7, 9, 10].

### Strategic Recommendations & Long-Term Outlook
To maximize the industrial potential of SIB technology over the next decade, stakeholders across the battery ecosystem should pursue targeted actions:

1. **Standardization of Balance-of-Plant (BOP) Integration:** System integrators must standardize containerized SIB pack architectures and battery management systems (BMS) tailored to sodium's voltage plateaus, accelerating the reduction of system-level installation premiums [3, 12].
2. **Advanced Electrolyte and Interphase Engineering:** R&D efforts should prioritize electrolyte formulations containing advanced fluorinated additives and ionic liquids that passivate hard carbon surfaces, suppressing metallic sodium reactivity above 50°C and extending high-temperature calendar life [7, 9, 16].
3. **Hybrid Cell/Pack Architectures:** Battery pack developers should deploy dual-chemistry hybrid packs (combining SIB and LIB/LFP cells within a single pack architecture). This hybrid approach leverages SIB's low-temperature power and cost benefits alongside LFP's high volumetric density, providing an optimized solution for all-weather electric vehicles [3, 12].
4. **Dual-Track Industrialization:** Cell manufacturers should maintain dual-capable gigafactory lines, preserving flexibility to scale SIB output during lithium price spikes while continuing LIB production for range-critical applications [11, 12].

In conclusion, Sodium-Ion Battery technology is positioned to secure a substantial share of global energy storage capacity [3, 13]. By establishing market leadership in stationary grid storage, cold-climate mobility, and micro-mobility, SIBs will diversify the global energy storage landscape, mitigating supply chain vulnerabilities while accelerating clean energy integration [3, 4, 12, 14].
